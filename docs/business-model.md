# Wayvee: The Revenue Architecture

*Business Model — First Edition — July 2026*

A staged, executable business model built from what already exists in Wayvee today — not a hypothetical. Each stage funds the next, and each one borrows its structure from a company that already proved it works.

> Don't ask anyone to pay you. Share what you're already earning. Every stage below follows from that one sentence.

The founder strategy memo behind this project made hotel-partnership SaaS the primary payer from day one — the one channel already flagged as hard for a solo founder to open cold. This model keeps every principle from that memo (guest-first, verified data, no dark patterns) but replaces its distribution dependency with a structure borrowed from how Booking.com and Expedia actually built their partner networks: give the partner a reason to say yes in five minutes, not five weeks.

| Stage | Who pays | Proven by | Unlocks when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Affiliate wedge | Guests, indirectly | Viator · GetYourGuide · Klook | Live today |
| 2. Rev-share placement | Nobody, out of pocket | Booking.com / Expedia affiliate network | One real distributor |
| 3. SaaS upsell | Properties, once proven | Toast · Mindbody | Stage 2 volume + numbers |
| 4. Data licensing | Venues / DMOs | Placer.ai · SafeGraph | Real usage history |

## The Diagnosis: Why Not Start With Hotel SaaS

Every version of this plan so far has treated "get a hotel to pay a subscription fee" as the starting move. Look at why that's the wrong first domino, not just a hard one.

A SaaS pitch to a hotel asks the buyer to do five things before a dollar changes hands: notice the email, get a meeting, get IT/procurement sign-off, get a budget line approved, and trust that a small, unproven company will still exist next quarter. Every one of those steps is a place the deal dies — and a solo founder doesn't have the sales capacity to run that process across enough properties to find the ones that say yes.

Compare that to what Booking.com and Expedia actually did to build the largest travel-distribution networks on earth. They didn't sell software to partner sites. They handed them a widget and said: *"put this on your page, and we'll split the commission whenever someone books through it. Costs you nothing. Changes nothing about your business. Pure upside."* That is a five-minute yes, not a five-week procurement cycle — because it asks for a favor, not a budget.

> The lesson isn't "avoid hotels." It's "don't make the first ask a subscription fee." Ask for shelf space instead, and pay for it out of money you're already making.

That reframe is the entire model in this document. Everything below is the same underlying business — verified local catalog, an AI concierge, official booking handoffs — restaged so the money starts moving before anyone has to trust a pitch deck.

## Stage 1 — The Affiliate Wedge

**Live today — no new sales required.** The part of the business model that needs nobody's permission, because it's already running.

Wayvee has been enrolled in Viator's affiliate program since July 22, 2026. Every tour and experience booking link a guest taps carries real commission-tracking parameters. Ticketmaster and TicketNetwork are verified for the same kind of program and pending only on a tracking-link format from their side. None of this required a single sales conversation — it's an application and an API key.

**The comparable:** GetYourGuide, Viator, and Klook are multi-billion-dollar companies built entirely on this mechanic — commission on a completed booking, paid by the supplier (the tour operator, the venue), never the guest. Nobody had to be sold anything to make this real; the guest just needed a better reason to tap through.

**What the AI concierge actually contributes here** is not a new revenue line — it's a conversion lift on the line that already exists. A guest handed one well-matched, already-explained plan is measurably more likely to tap a booking link than a guest scrolling four browse tabs looking for one. The entire AI build (the Build Book) pays for itself by lifting Stage 1's existing take rate, before Stage 2 or 3 ever need to exist.

| Mechanic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Who pays | The supplier (Viator, eventually Ticketmaster/TicketNetwork) — never the guest, never the property |
| Wayvee's cut | A commission percentage set by each program's own terms |
| What's needed to grow it | More guest sessions, better-matched recommendations — not more partners |
| Dependency | None. This runs today regardless of anything else in this document. |

## Stage 2 — The Rev-Share Placement

**The actual unlock.** Trade a subscription ask for a split of money that already exists — the single change that makes distribution tractable for one person.

The pitch to a bar, a venue, an independent inn, or eventually a hotel is not "pay us monthly." It's: **"put our QR code where your guests already look for something to do — we split the commission we earn from bookings your guests make, with you. It costs you nothing either way."** There's no invoice, no procurement, no IT integration, no risk if it doesn't work — only upside if it does.

**The comparable:** this is structurally identical to how Booking.com and Expedia recruited their affiliate network of partner travel sites, and to how airlines and hotels run co-branded credit-card revenue splits — align the partner's incentive with yours instead of asking them to underwrite you before you've proven anything.

Why this is the right Stage 2, specifically for a solo founder:

- It's a five-minute conversation, not a sales cycle — there's no budget line to fight for.
- It requires zero technical integration on the partner's side — a printed card and a QR code, the same guest-card artifact already produced for the pilot, is the entire ask.
- It's the exact "one real, founder-reachable distributor" step the Build Book's Part 5.2 already calls for — this just gives that step commercial terms instead of leaving it as a favor.
- It produces the first real numbers (sessions, conversion, guest satisfaction) that make Stage 3 an easy next conversation instead of a cold pitch.

| Mechanic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Who pays | Nobody, out of pocket — this is a split of Stage 1's existing commission |
| The ask | Shelf space (a card, a QR code, a mention at check-in) in exchange for a revenue share |
| What's needed to grow it | One relationship at a time — a bar, a venue, an independent inn |
| Dependency | Stage 1 must already be generating real commission to split |

### The Stage 2 Pitch, Written Out

For the front desk, the bar owner, or the innkeeper — adapt the specifics, keep the structure: no cost, no commitment, real upside.

> "We built a free tool that helps your guests figure out dinner and something to do tonight in Oakland — real places, real hours, nothing invented. All we're asking is for this card at your front desk or bar. When a guest books a tour, a ticket, or an experience through it, we earn a small commission — and we'll split that with you. It costs you nothing, there's nothing to install, and if it doesn't work for your guests, just take the card down. Want to try it for a month?"

What makes this pitch work, mechanically:

- It leads with the guest benefit, not the revenue split — the split is the second sentence, not the first.
- "Take the card down" removes all perceived risk — there's no contract to get out of.
- "A month" is a trial framing, not a commitment — this matters enormously for a relationship-based first yes.
- It never asks the partner to change anything about how they operate — the card is the entire integration.

## Stage 3 — The SaaS Upsell

**Earned, not pitched.** The subscription ask the original strategy memo wanted from day one — moved to the point where it's an easy yes instead of a leap of faith.

Once Stage 2 has real numbers — sessions, plan-acceptance rate, an estimate of staff time saved answering "what should I do tonight" questions — offer a flat monthly fee for a reporting dashboard and priority placement, for partners who want more than a rev-share line item. This is not a new pitch; it's a formalization of something already visibly working.

**The comparable:** Toast and Mindbody both built durable hospitality SaaS businesses by landing with something free or low-friction and expanding into paid tiers only after the operator had already seen value — never by cold-selling the subscription first. Slack's own land-free-expand-to-paid motion is the same shape outside hospitality.

| Mechanic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Who pays | The property, now with a real ROI number to justify the line item |
| The ask | A flat monthly fee for a dashboard, reporting, and priority placement |
| What's needed to grow it | A repeatable pitch built from Stage 2's actual results, property by property |
| Dependency | Stage 2 must have run long enough to produce a real, defensible number |

## Stage 4 — The Data Moat

**The part a competitor can't fast-follow.** The structural advantage that rivals how the largest tech companies actually defend their businesses — not the app itself, but what compounds underneath it.

A competing app can copy a UI in a weekend. It cannot copy months of real, specific "what did guests in Downtown Oakland actually ask for tonight, and what did they actually book" data — because that only exists by having run the product with real guests for real time. This is the moat: not the catalog, not the AI, but the proprietary demand signal that accumulates underneath both.

**The comparable:** Placer.ai and SafeGraph built entire businesses out of exactly this insight — location and behavior data, licensed to the parties who need to understand real-world demand (retailers, cities, investors), monetized as a business distinct from whatever product originally generated the data.

For Wayvee, the buyer for this layer isn't a guest or a single property — it's a venue that wants to know what's actually driving foot traffic on a given night, or a destination marketing organization (a city or regional tourism board) that currently has no real-time signal on visitor intent at all. This is explicitly a later-stage line, gated on Stage 2/3 producing enough real history to be worth licensing — named here so it isn't forgotten, not because it's a near-term priority.

> Every other stage in this document could, in principle, be copied by a well-funded competitor in a quarter. This one can't — it only exists after the fact, and only for whoever actually ran the product.

## A Simple Unit-Economics Sketch

*Illustrative planning estimates, not measured data — replace every figure with a real one the moment there's real usage to measure. The point is the shape of the calculation.*

| Line item | Illustrative estimate | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| AI cost per generated plan | A few cents | Cost, all stages |
| Commission per completed Viator/ticket booking | A real percentage, set by each program | 1 |
| Wayvee's share of a rev-share split | Negotiated per partner, a minority share to the partner | 2 |
| Flat monthly SaaS fee, once earned | Priced against staff time saved, not guesswork | 3 |
| Data licensing fee, once real history exists | Priced against what a DMO currently pays for visitor insight | 4 |

The question this answers isn't "how much will this make" — nobody can honestly say that yet. It's **"does each stage's cost stay small relative to what it unlocks."** Stage 1's AI cost per plan should stay far below the commission a single accepted plan generates. Stage 2's revenue given away in the split should stay far below what it costs to acquire the same distribution through paid channels. If either ratio inverts, that's the signal to revisit the stage before scaling it further — not a reason to abandon it.
